### Nashville, but make it Ween 🎙️

Released in 1996, *12 Golden Country Greats* came from one of Ween’s boldest left turns. Aaron Freeman and Mickey Melchiondo had already built a name on scrappy, strange alternative rock that could sound homemade, warped and gloriously juvenile. For this record, they went to **Bradley’s Barn** in Tennessee, a studio with deep country history, and recorded with seasoned Nashville players under producer **Ben Vaughn**.

That decision matters. This is not Ween fooling around with a few country jokes in the rehearsal room. They hired the real thing, including figures from Nashville’s old guard such as **Hargus “Pig” Robbins**, **Charlie McCoy**, **Buddy Spicher**, **Buddy Harman**, and even **the Jordanaires**. One of the album’s great stories is that many of the instrumental tracks were cut straight, with the session musicians treating the songs as proper country recordings, while Ween later added vocals and their own lyrical mischief. It gave the album its strange split personality, immaculate country craft meeting sly, filthy, absurd Ween humour.

Your Rhino vinyl makes that process even clearer. The remastered original album already feels tight and focused at ten tracks, but the **Golden Demos** and **Outtakes & Demos** let you hear the songs before the full Nashville polish settled on them.

### Country played dead straight 🎸

What makes the album distinctive is how sincerely it handles the genre. The guitars twang with real Nashville bite, the rhythm section sits deep in the pocket, and the arrangements lean on pedal steel, fiddle, piano and warm backing vocals. There is no wink in the playing. The wink is in the writing.

Songs such as **“I’m Holding You”** and **“Help Me Scrape the Mucus Off My Brain”** have genuine ache, while **“Piss Up a Rope”** and **“Mister Richard Smoker”** turn that classic barroom setting into something grubby and hilarious. The guitar work is especially important here. Rather than fuzzy alt-rock attack, the album uses clipped rhythm parts, clean leads and tasteful bends that come from country discipline. The drums and bass never push for rock heft. They glide, shuffle and swing.

### A country detour in the grunge years 🌵

In the middle of the 1990s alternative boom, this was a wonderfully awkward move. While post-grunge and loud guitar records crowded the shelves, Ween made a traditionalist country album with elite session musicians. That fit the decade’s appetite for genre-hopping, but it also pushed against the idea that alternative rock had to sound abrasive or miserable.

The DIY spirit never vanished, either. Even with Elektra money and a famous studio, the impulse was still stubbornly indie: follow the odd idea, commit completely, and do not tidy it up for the market.

### Reception, cult status and the “12” joke 📀

The album puzzled some listeners at first, but it has aged beautifully. Critics and fans have come to treat it as one of Ween’s sharpest records, partly because it is both parody and homage at once. It never collapses into a cheap joke, because the music is too well made.

And yes, the title joke still lands. The original LP had **ten** songs, not twelve. The extra tracks that appear on later archival editions, and on your expanded set, finally flesh out the missing pieces. That makes this reissue more than a nostalgia item. It lets you hear *12 Golden Country Greats* as a bigger world, one where demos, throwaways and outtakes prove that Ween’s weird Nashville trip was far more than a prank.

### Recording the room, not just the songs 🎙️

Lambchop’s *Punching the Clown* arrived on 21 August 2026 as the band’s seventeenth studio album, released in Europe by City Slang. It was recorded between August and December 2025, with the main sessions taking place at April Base in Wisconsin, Justin Vernon’s studio. Merge described the core tracking as happening over just three days, which helps explain the album’s particular feel, it has the concentration of a live ensemble record, even though its details were refined later.

Kurt Wagner wrote the songs with Andrew Broder, and Ryan Olson produced. That trio matters. Wagner brings the dry, humane writing and softly bruised singing, Broder adds wiry texture and melodic unease, and Olson gives the whole thing shape without sanding away its odd corners. Vernon also appears on banjo, and there is a six-part choir woven through the record. Those ingredients could have produced something grand. Instead, Lambchop keep it intimate.

That intimacy feels linked to the post-pandemic years. Even though this was not made as a purely remote album, it carries lessons from that period. Lambchop had already spent recent years working in more fragmented, file-swapping ways, and here they fold that sensibility back into a record that still sounds like people sharing a space.

### Folk, gospel, art rock, and a little interference 🌫️

What makes *Punching the Clown* distinctive is how calmly it mixes forms. There is folk in the storytelling, country in the phrasing, gospel in the choir, indie rock in the arrangement sense, and art rock in the way small disruptions keep appearing. Reviews kept returning to that contrast, warm harmonies on one side, strange noise and friction on the other.

The guitar work is a big part of that. Broder’s playing tends to be clean, restrained and slightly off-centre, less about riffs than atmosphere. Vernon’s banjo adds an American roots colour, but used sparingly, almost like texture rather than ornament. Underneath, the rhythm section stays patient. The drums and bass rarely push forward; they give the songs a slow pulse and leave room for Wagner’s voice, which is much less processed here than on some recent Lambchop records.

### America under the skin 🇺🇸

For such a gentle-sounding album, *Punching the Clown* has a sharp political edge. Song titles like “White People”, “Andrew Jackson Asshat” and “The New World Wave” make that plain. Wagner is not writing protest songs in the old sense. He is more interested in how history sits inside daily life, in family stories, civic myths, race, money, and the long afterlife of American violence.

“The New World Wave” drew particular praise for its sweeping sense of memory and survival. It is one of those Lambchop songs that sounds modest at first, then slowly reveals how much social history it is carrying.

### Reception and early legacy 📰

The album was very warmly received. Pitchfork gave it Best New Music, while MOJO and other reviewers praised the return to a close, human Lambchop sound after the more electronically altered vocal approach of recent years. Critics also liked how it reconnects the band’s early alt-country and chamber-pop instincts with their later experimental habits.

It is too new to talk about full legacy, but it already feels like an important late-period Lambchop album, one that shows how genre fluidity in the 2020s can sound thoughtful rather than fashionable. This is art rock that whispers, folk music with static in the signal, and a band still finding new ways to sound like themselves.

## Cansei de Ser Sexy by Cansei de Ser Sexy 💋

### Recording it like a party 🎛️

CSS’s debut arrived first in Brazil in 2005, before Sub Pop took it to the US in 2006 and Sire handled the UK in 2007. That release path tells you a lot about the record itself. This was not a polished major-label launch. It grew out of São Paulo nightlife, internet buzz and a band whose early instinct was, in Adriano Cintra’s words, to throw a party first and sort the rest out later.

Cintra produced the album, and the sessions appear to have taken place in 2005 in a home-made setting rather than a grand studio. You can hear that in the sound. The drum machines feel cheap on purpose, the synths squeak and jab, and the guitars often sound scratched up rather than smoothed out. Instead of hiding budget limits, CSS turned them into personality. The record feels like a group of friends discovering how far style, rhythm and nerve can take a song.

### Trashy pop, punk nerves and electro chaos ⚡

What makes *Cansei de Ser Sexy* memorable is its gleeful clash of scenes and moods. It pulls from dance-punk, electroclash, indie rock and bratty punk-pop, then throws them together with almost cartoonish confidence. Songs such as “Alala” and “Music Is My Hot Hot Sex” run on repetition, chant vocals and hooks that feel half seduction, half joke.

That mix suited the mid-2000s perfectly. CSS were plugged into the decade’s obsession with irony, fashion, nightlife and cool-as-performance. Even the band name, “I’m Tired of Being Sexy”, sounds like a slogan scribbled on a flyer at 2 am. Beneath the joke, though, there is a sharp instinct for rhythm and shape. The album never sounds academic. It sounds physical, social and a little unruly.

### From blog-era buzz to global indie export 🌍

The album’s rise also belongs to the digital turn. CSS came up at the moment when online music portals, blogs and file-sharing chatter could push a local band into overseas conversation very quickly. Their link with Trama and Trama Virtual helped, and once tastemakers outside Brazil caught on, the old industry gatekeepers mattered less than they had a few years earlier.

Commercially, this was no blockbuster, but it travelled well. The album charted in the UK and found a place on the US dance chart, which is impressive for a Portuguese-speaking Brazilian indie band making knowingly scrappy club-pop. Critics were mostly warm, even when they found the record abrasive. Reviews often praised its energy, cheek and fresh messiness.

### Why it still matters 🎉

CSS’s debut captures a very specific millennial mood after 9/11: restless, ironic, pleasure-seeking and suspicious of earnest rock seriousness. Rather than addressing world events head-on, it turns towards escape, bodies, parties and self-invention.

Its legacy lives in that DIY confidence. This album proved that a band could sound homemade, look stylish, cross borders online and still keep its odd edges intact. It remains one of the clearest snapshots of blog-era indie at its most fun and most chaotic.

## The Division Bell by Pink Floyd 🔔

### Recording it back together 🎛️
For this 2019 European vinyl reissue on Parlophone and Pink Floyd Records, you’re hearing *The Division Bell* in its full double-LP flow, from the drifting instrumental opener “Cluster One” to the long goodbye of “High Hopes”. The album itself was made in 1993 and released in 1994, recorded across Britannia Row, Abbey Road, Metropolis, The Creek, and David Gilmour’s Astoria houseboat studio. That mix of big-room professionalism and private working space suits the music perfectly. It sounds polished, but never stiff.

What mattered most in the sessions was a return to a more shared way of working. Gilmour, Nick Mason and Richard Wright shaped the record with producer Bob Ezrin, and Wright’s presence feels especially important. His keyboards give the album much of its warmth and melancholy. Guy Pratt, though not a formal band member on the sleeve, helped lock in the bass foundation. The title came from the idea of failed communication, and that theme runs through nearly every side.

### The sound, space, and slow burn 🎸
This is Pink Floyd in late-period mode: expansive prog rock, arena rock, ambient textures, bluesy guitar phrasing, and songs that unfold rather than rush. “What Do You Want From Me” has a harder edge, but much of the album prefers atmosphere and patient build. “Marooned”, which later won a Grammy for Best Rock Instrumental Performance, is almost pure mood, carried by Gilmour’s singing guitar tone.

The guitar work defines the emotional centre. Gilmour plays with restraint, aiming for feeling over speed, and that choice gives tracks like “Coming Back to Life” and “High Hopes” their weight. Beneath him, Mason’s drumming is measured and roomy rather than busy, while Wright fills the gaps with organ, synths and chord colours that make the whole record breathe.

### Reception, reputation, and the long afterlife 🌙
On release, *The Division Bell* was a huge commercial success, hitting number one in both the UK and the US. Critics were more divided. Some heard a beautifully made, thoughtful Pink Floyd album, others thought it was too smooth and too safe beside the band’s 1970s peak. Over time, though, the record has aged well. “High Hopes” in particular has become one of the most admired songs of the Gilmour-led era.

Its legacy is tied to that mood of reflection. This is not a confrontational record like some Roger Waters-era albums. It is inward-looking, concerned with distance, regret, memory and the things people fail to say.

### From 1994 to the streaming era 📱
Although the album belongs to the 1990s, the 2019 vinyl reissue shows how well it fits modern listening habits. Streaming has kept “High Hopes”, “Keep Talking” and “Coming Back to Life” in circulation for younger listeners, while social media and collector culture have turned physical reissues into events of their own. The blue-vinyl anniversary edition gave fans something tactile and beautiful, while the songs continued their second life online.

That double existence suits *The Division Bell*. It is spacious enough for headphones, cinematic enough for playlists, and still best when you let all four sides unfold in order.

## Exterminator (XTRMNTR) by Primal Scream ⚡

### Recording in the bunker 🛠️

Primal Scream made *XTRMNTR* as if they were building a pirate transmission. The album grew out of the band’s late-1990s bunker sessions, with Bobby Gillespie and Andrew Innes pushing far beyond standard rock recording. This was not a neat “band plays songs in a room” record. Tracks were assembled, reshaped and attacked from different angles, with remix culture folded straight into the album itself.

That is why your vinyl’s running order feels so striking. It includes both the Jagz Kooner and Chemical Brothers versions of “Swastika Eyes”, which tells you a lot about the project. Primal Scream treated the remix as part of the main text, not an extra. Kevin Shields also entered the picture, adding his blown-out guitar language to “MBV Arkestra (If They Move Kill ’Em)”. The result is a studio album that behaves like a collision between a rock LP, a sound system dub plate and a bag of dangerous 12-inch singles.

### A brutal mix of rock, electro and noise 🎛️

What makes *XTRMNTR* distinctive is how hard it hits while refusing to settle into one genre. “Accelerator” is all clipped violence, distorted guitar and forward motion. “Kill All Hippies” opens with a thick, narcotic pulse. “Blood Money” and “Shoot Speed / Kill Light” run on menace, repetition and pressure rather than tidy hooks.

The guitar work matters here because it often acts as texture and force, not decoration. Shields’ contribution is famous, but the whole album treats guitar as a smear, a siren or a blast of static. Underneath that, the rhythm section locks into grooves borrowed from techno, dub, hip-hop and industrial music. The drums often feel programmed even when they are not, and the bass keeps everything heavy and hypnotic. That mechanical drive is a huge part of the album’s personality.

### Millennial dread, politics and the digital age 📡

Released at the start of 2000, *XTRMNTR* caught the mood of a new century turning ugly. Its themes are paranoia, control, corporate power, chemical escape and violent authority. “Swastika Eyes” is the obvious flashpoint, a furious attack on modern power dressed in slogans sharp enough to scandalise.

It arrived before 9/11, but its mood soon felt eerily suited to the years that followed. Surveillance, militarised politics and permanent crisis all seem to hover over it. Sonically, the album also feels tied to the digital turn. Its cut-up structure, remix logic and harsh signal-processing belong to a moment when rock was absorbing club culture and computer-based production more deeply than before.

### Reception, legacy and a changing industry 📰

Critics were strong on it at the time, and it reached No. 3 in the UK, which is remarkable for such an abrasive record. It has since grown in stature, often treated as one of Primal Scream’s boldest albums and one of the fiercest records to come out of the post-Britpop hangover.

There is also an industry footnote that suits its mood. *XTRMNTR* was effectively tied to the end of the Creation era, with “Accelerator” becoming the label’s final single. So the album caught two endings at once: the fade of old indie structures, and the start of a digital century that would change how music was made, remixed and heard. On this 2026 European Sony vinyl, that chaos still sounds gloriously toxic.

## Temple Of Low Men by Crowded House 🎧

### 🎙️ Made fast, felt deep

Released in the UK on 11 July 1988 through Capitol, *Temple Of Low Men* was Crowded House’s second album, and it took a striking turn away from the easy sparkle of the debut. Neil Finn brought in a set of songs that were more troubled, inward-looking and emotionally tangled. The band recorded across 1987 and 1988, with producer Mitchell Froom returning, alongside engineer Tchad Blake and mixer Bob Clearmountain. That team mattered. Froom had a gift for odd textures and atmosphere, while Clearmountain gave the finished record radio clarity without sanding off its mood.

One lovely detail about the album is how compact it is. Your UK vinyl runs just ten tracks, and the sequencing is brilliantly sly. Side A opens with “I Feel Possessed”, a strange, uneasy song that tells you at once this is not a simple hit-chasing follow-up. Then Side B closes with “Better Be Home Soon”, the warm, aching ballad that became the album’s best-known single. Putting the obvious crowd-pleaser at the very end feels deliberate, almost like a reward after the record’s emotional shadows.

### 🎹 New wave tools, rock heart

Although Crowded House often get filed under pop rock, alternative rock or new wave, this album does something more interesting than any single label suggests. The songs are built on guitars, bass, drums and Finn’s melodic instinct, but the production uses keyboards and studio effects in a subtle way. Froom’s synths and keys do not dominate in the big, glossy late-80s style. Instead, they haunt the edges of the songs, adding colour, tension and little flashes of unease.

That is a huge part of what makes *Temple Of Low Men* distinctive. “Into Temptation” drifts with a near-dreamlike pull, “Mansion In The Slums” mixes beauty with bite, and “In The Lowlands” has a bruised, reflective quality that feels miles away from standard arena rock. Crowded House were working in the MTV era, but they did not shape the album around brute-force choruses and oversized production. They made something polished, yet intimate.

### 📺 Between MTV and the alternative crowd

The late 80s wanted strong singles, memorable videos and a recognisable image, and Crowded House could do all of that. Several songs from the album were pushed as singles, and “Better Be Home Soon” became a major calling card. Yet the record itself resists the usual commercial blueprint. It keeps a moody centre, and that helped it sit in an interesting space between mainstream pop success and the more thoughtful edge of alternative rock.

That balance is part of why the album still feels fresh. It has hooks, but it also has ambiguity, odd corners and grown-up emotional weight.

### 🏆 Reception and afterlife

The album was warmly reviewed and did especially well in Australia and New Zealand, reaching No. 1 in Australia and earning major ARIA recognition, including Album of the Year. In the US, it reached the Billboard Top 40, though it sold less than the debut. Even so, its reputation has only grown.

For many listeners, this is where Crowded House fully became Crowded House: elegant songwriting, melancholy without self-pity, and rock music unafraid of tenderness. It bends rock convention by making sensitivity, doubt and atmosphere the main event.

## Greatest Hits by The Doors 🚪

### 🎙️ A compilation built from several eras
Although your copy is a 2026 European Elektra vinyl pressing, *Greatest Hits* began life in 1980 as a single-LP Doors compilation, a neat distillation of recordings made between 1966 and 1971. That matters, because this album was not created in one burst. It pulls from very different sessions and moods: the tightly controlled Paul A. Rothchild productions of the first albums, then the looser, more lived-in sound of *L.A. Woman*, where Bruce Botnick and the band took charge after Rothchild bowed out.

You can hear that shift in the running order. Side A moves from the sharp, pop-leaning snap of “Hello, I Love You” into the full seven-minute “Light My Fire”, then through the eerie character study of “People Are Strange” and the sly blues of “Love Me Two Times”, before landing in the rain-soaked drift of “Riders On The Storm”. Side B is rougher and earthier, opening with “Break On Through” and driving toward “Roadhouse Blues” and “L.A. Woman”. For a ten-track set, it maps the band’s journey very well.

### 🎹 Why these songs still sound so distinctive
The Doors always sounded unlike their peers because the organ sat at the centre. Ray Manzarek’s keyboards gave them a haunted, fluid quality, whether he was pushing “Light My Fire” into modal improvisation or sketching the noir atmosphere of “Riders On The Storm” on electric piano. Without a standard full-time bassist in the early years, the group had more space in the arrangement, and that space became part of the drama.

The blues strain is everywhere here. “Roadhouse Blues” works like a barroom shuffle, “Love Me Two Times” leans on blues phrasing and innuendo, and “L.A. Woman” turns the road song into something feverish and urban. Robbie Krieger’s guitar lines borrow from Chicago and Delta traditions, but twist them through psychedelic rock. Jim Morrison, meanwhile, sings like a poet trying on the voice of a blues shouter, half crooner, half prophet.

### 📈 Reception, catalogue life, and the digital age
This compilation became a reliable catalogue seller, going multi-platinum in the US and helping introduce new listeners to the band long after Morrison’s death. Critics have sometimes preferred broader anthologies, since *Greatest Hits* leaves out major epics such as “The End”, but its brevity is part of its appeal. It gets to the point.

The digital era changed how people met this album. CD editions added extra tracks, and later remasters reshaped the sound for newer formats. In the streaming age, *Greatest Hits* almost becomes a playlist before playlists existed, a compact route into the band’s world.

### 🌩️ Legacy and modern resonance
Because this is a compilation of late-60s and early-70s material, post-9/11 or millennial themes are not intentional. Still, the songs speak uncannily to later unease. “People Are Strange” captures alienation, “Not To Touch The Earth” feels apocalyptic, and “Riders On The Storm” turns danger into weather. That is one reason The Doors keep returning.

What lasts most is their blend of blues, theatre, poetry and menace. *Greatest Hits* is not the whole story, but it is a very persuasive version of it.

## The Colour Of Spring by Talk Talk 🌿

    layout={{

### A turning point in the studio 🎛️

Although your copy is the handsome 2012 UK and Europe vinyl-and-DVD reissue on EMI, the music itself comes from a remarkable 1985 recording run, released first in 1986. This was the album where Talk Talk stopped thinking like a synth-pop chart act and started chasing something far stranger and more human. Mark Hollis and producer Tim Friese-Greene spent months writing before the band proper came in, then shaped the record through long sessions at Battery Studios in London.

What changed was the method. Hollis spoke about wanting to work with musicians first, not machines. So instead of building everything around sequencers and bright electronic surfaces, Talk Talk brought in a large cast of players and leaned into piano, organ, harmonica, acoustic bass, percussion and room sound. You can hear that shift immediately on “Happiness Is Easy”, which feels spacious and tactile in a way few mid-80s pop records did.

### The sound, and why it still feels special 🎹

The Colour Of Spring sits in a fascinating spot between pop, rock, jazz and something harder to pin down. “Life’s What You Make It” is the obvious entry point, with its pounding piano figure and slow-building tension, but the album keeps slipping away from neat labels. “Living In Another World” has a restless, circling groove, while “April 5th” and “Chameleon Day” hint at the stillness and mystery Talk Talk would pursue later on Spirit Of Eden.

That is what makes it distinctive. The songs are structured enough to stay memorable, but the arrangements breathe. Lee Harris’s drumming avoids the big gated thump that dated so many records from the period. Guest players such as Danny Thompson on acoustic bass and Mark Feltham on harmonica add warmth and grain. Tim Friese-Greene’s keyboard work, including Kurzweil electric piano, sits inside the ensemble rather than dominating it.

### Reception, success and the road ahead 📈

This was Talk Talk’s commercial high point, helped by the success of “Life’s What You Make It”. It reached a wide audience while quietly moving away from the band’s earlier image. Critics have since treated it as the missing link in the group’s story, the record that joins It’s My Life to the more austere Spirit Of Eden and Laughing Stock.

Its legacy is larger than its chart run. Without this album’s success, EMI would never have given Talk Talk the freedom and budget for the records that later influenced post-rock, art rock and ambient-minded bands. In that sense, The Colour Of Spring changed music by opening a door rather than kicking one down.

### Why the 2012 edition matters now 💿

The 2012 reissue suits the streaming age in an odd way. Talk Talk were never built for social media chatter or playlist skimming, yet this edition invites slow listening: heavyweight vinyl, a high-resolution DVD, and the addition of “It’s Getting Late In The Evening”, a period B-side that fits the album’s mood beautifully. In a time when music often arrives chopped into clips and recommendations, The Colour Of Spring still asks for patience, silence and full attention. That may be why it feels so modern.

### 🎛️ Exile, movement, and the making of the record

*Losey* arrives after a major change in Gnoomes’ life.
