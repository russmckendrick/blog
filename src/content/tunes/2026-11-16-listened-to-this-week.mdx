### A wake, a songbook, and a very Irish act of remembrance 🍀
This Rubyworks triple LP is less a tidy tribute album than a huge communal gathering around Shane MacGowan’s writing. It was put together by Victoria Mary Clarke with Siobhán MacGowan, and that matters, because the record feels personal rather than corporate. The idea was not simply to round up stars for the obvious hits, but to present MacGowan as a songwriter whose catalogue reaches far beyond a few canonical Pogues anthems.

Because it is a various-artists set, there is no single recording story. These performances were cut in different studios, with different producers, and with each act leaning into its own strengths. That scattered method suits the material. MacGowan wrote songs sturdy enough to survive being pushed towards folk balladry, indie scruff, pub-rock roar, or worn-down late-night crooning.

### Who shares the sleeve, and what they bring 🎤
The headline names are hard to ignore. Bruce Springsteen opening with **“A Rainy Night In Soho”** gives the set immediate weight, treating one of MacGowan’s tenderest songs with the kind of weathered grandeur Springsteen does so well. Hozier on **“Fairytale Of New York”** brings modern Irish star power to the album’s most famous composition. U2, Tom Waits, Steve Earle, Primal Scream, The Jesus And Mary Chain, Bob Geldof and Glen Hansard all make sense because each has some kinship with MacGowan’s mix of romance, wreckage and defiance.

Just as interesting are the less expected guests. Kingfishr, Amble, Lyra, Madra Salach, Pinch Of Snuff, Garron Noone and Tom Creagh help stop the record from turning into a heritage exercise. They pull the songs back into the present tense.

### What the track selection is trying to catch 🕯️
The selection maps the world MacGowan wrote about: the Irish in London, old sorrows carried into noisy bars, politics, exile, comic bravado, doomed love, and the half-mythic swagger of the Pogues era. You get the street-level bruising of **“The Old Main Drag”**, the political anger of **“Streets Of Sorrow / Birmingham Six”**, the communal lift of **“Streams Of Whiskey”** and **“Bottle Of Smoke”**, and the dreamier ache of **“The Broad Majestic Shannon”** and **“Rain Street”**.

That spread is what makes the album distinctive. It reminds you that MacGowan was never only the ragged poet of drunken singalongs. He could be savage, funny, devotional and heartbreakingly soft within the same body of work.

### How the running order works, and the obvious draws 📀
Your pressing’s sequence is smartly paced across six sides. Side A starts with prestige names and familiar emotional territory. Side B leans into rowdier pub-floor energy. Side C balances canon and curveballs, with **The Pogues’ “Gabrielle”** acting as a direct line back to the source. Side D gets darker and stranger. Side E turns reflective and atmospheric, especially with Tom Waits on **“Summer In Sia”**. Side F feels like the after-hours finish, intimate and a little haunted.

The obvious draws are Springsteen, Hozier, U2, Tom Waits, Damien Dempsey and Glen Hansard. The unexpected inclusions are often the ones that make the set worth living with: **Madra Salach doing “Turkish Song Of The Damned”**, **Garron Noone on “White City”**, and **Kate Moss singing “Lonesome Highway”**. Early reviews praised the ambition, though some felt the length was a lot to take in at once. Still, that sprawl feels right. MacGowan’s world was never neat, and this record wisely refuses to tidy it up.

### A revived compilation, not a lost new album 📀

Despite the title sounding like a fresh statement, *The World According To Clash* is a 2026 Sony Music vinyl compilation for the UK and Ireland, not a newly recorded Clash studio album. That matters, because the story here is really about curation. This LP pulls together eleven songs from across the band’s peak years and turns them into a compact map of what made The Clash such a restless, unpredictable group.

The running order is smart. Side A moves from the swagger of “Rock The Casbah” into “Clamp Down”, “Train In Vain” and “Brand New Cadillac”, then lands on the wiry funk of “The Magnificent Seven”. Side B answers with “I Fought The Law”, “The Guns Of Brixton”, “Spanish Bomb”, “London Calling”, “Lost In The Supermarket” and “Should I Stay Or Should I Go”. It plays less like a greatest hits package and more like a guided tour through their many musical identities.

### How the original recordings were made 🛠️

Most of this set draws from the sessions for *London Calling*, *Sandinista!* and *Combat Rock*, which is why it covers so much ground. The *London Calling* material came from a period when the band, with producer Guy Stevens and engineer Bill Price, stretched far beyond first-wave punk. They were still fierce, but they were also making room for detail, melody and atmosphere.

That is where songs like “Clamp Down”, “Spanish Bomb”, “Lost In The Supermarket” and “Train In Vain” took shape. “The Guns Of Brixton”, written and sung by Paul Simonon, brought reggae to the front of the band’s sound. “The Magnificent Seven” from *Sandinista!* pushed even further, folding in funk, dub and early hip-hop ideas. Then *Combat Rock* delivered “Rock The Casbah” and “Should I Stay Or Should I Go”, songs with sharper pop edges but no loss of attitude.

There is no pandemic creation story here. This release is archival in nature, built from classic recordings rather than remote sessions or post-Covid collaboration.

### Why it still sounds so distinctive 🔊

What makes The Clash special is how natural their genre-hopping feels. Punk is the spine, but these songs bring in rock and roll, reggae, dub, funk and pop without sounding like a history lesson. Mick Jones often supplied the melodic sparkle and lead hooks, while Joe Strummer drove the rhythm guitar with that clipped, urgent attack. Underneath them, Paul Simonon’s bass and Topper Headon’s drumming gave the band its elasticity.

You can hear that immediately. “The Guns Of Brixton” leans on bass first. “The Magnificent Seven” lives on groove. “Clamp Down” snaps forward on rhythm guitar and drums. “Rock The Casbah” rides a danceable beat while still sounding scrappy enough to be The Clash.

### Reception, legacy and present-day bite 🌍

The songs gathered here were central to The Clash’s reputation, with *London Calling* in particular long treated as one of rock’s landmark records. “Rock The Casbah” and “Should I Stay Or Should I Go” became major crossover hits, but the deeper cuts are just as important to their legacy.

What hits hardest now is the subject matter. “Clamp Down” is about work, power and obedience. “London Calling” still feels full of dread and public disorder. “Lost In The Supermarket” catches consumer alienation with eerie precision. “Spanish Bomb” and “The Guns Of Brixton” keep history, violence and inequality in view.

So this 2026 compilation fits neatly into today’s taste for genre fluidity, but it also reminds you that The Clash were doing that decades ago, with better songs and sharper instincts than most.

### 🎙️ A breakthrough album, and a farewell

Sublime’s self-titled album arrived in July 1996, but it carries a tragic weight because Bradley Nowell died of a heroin overdose just two months before release. That gives the record an eerie double life. It is the band’s major-label leap, recorded with bigger resources, and also the last proper studio album by the original trio of Nowell, Eric Wilson and Bud Gaugh.

Much of it was recorded at Willie Nelson’s Pedernales Studio in Texas, with Paul Leary of Butthole Surfers helping shape its wiry, unruly sound. David Kahne also brought a cleaner, radio-ready touch. What makes the creation story so compelling is that Sublime did not smooth themselves into a standard alt-rock band once the budget increased. The songs still feel loose, funny, grubby and unpredictable, as if the DIY Long Beach spirit kept barging into the control room.

Your 2016 US double-vinyl reissue on Gasoline Alley, Skunk, Geffen and UMe preserves that messy brilliance beautifully, ending with “Doin’ Time” after a sequence that moves from “Garden Grove” and “What I Got” through “Santeria”, “Pawn Shop” and “Caress Me Down”.

### 🥁 Punk, ska, reggae, hip-hop, and whatever else fit

This album is often filed under ska-punk, but that is only half the story. Sublime pull together punk speed, reggae basslines, dub space, surf-side slouch, dancehall phrasing and hip-hop rhythm in a way that still feels oddly modern. “Same in the End” and “Seed” hit with punk bite, while “Doin’ Time” drifts through a smoky reggae-pop groove. “What I Got” sounds almost casual, until you realise how perfectly built it is.

That genre-mixing is a big part of why the album has lasted. It does not sound like a band trying on styles for novelty. It sounds like the natural result of Southern California radio, backyard parties, punk clubs and sound-system culture all crashing into one another.

### 📻 Hit singles, hard edges, and long afterlife

Commercially, this was massive. The album reached No. 13 on the Billboard 200, spent well over two years on the chart, and went multi-platinum in the US. “What I Got”, “Santeria”, “Wrong Way” and “Doin’ Time” became defining 90s alternative tracks.

Critically, people responded to the hooks and personality, though the album has always carried rough edges. Songs about addiction, exploitation, street life and violence can feel queasy on purpose, but some lyrics also belong to a very laddish 90s culture that modern listeners are more willing to question. That tension matters. Sublime were not tidy social commentators. They wrote from inside the chaos.

### 🛹 Why it still feels alive now

In the streaming era, Sublime has thrived because the songs work both as individual staples and as part of a larger myth. “Santeria” and “What I Got” live on playlists, in short-form clips, at parties, and in endless nostalgia loops online. At the same time, the full album still rewards a proper front-to-back listen because its world is so specific: anti-authority, locally rooted, funny, sad, stoned, and half a step from falling apart.

It also catches a 90s America shaped by unrest, distrust and cultural mash-up. “April 29, 1992 (Miami)” points directly at the LA uprising and its aftermath, while the whole album carries a feeling that official systems have failed and people are left to improvise their own code. That DIY streak is the heart of Sublime. Even when the band hit the mainstream, they still sounded like they were playing by their own rules.

### From thrash labyrinths to stadium force 🎛️  
Your 2014 US Blackened vinyl reissue captures an album first released in 1991, but it still feels like a turning point you can drop a needle on and hear within seconds. After the knotty, long-form writing of *…And Justice for All*, Metallica wanted songs that hit harder through clarity rather than sheer complication. Enter producer Bob Rock, who pushed the band far outside its comfort zone. Sessions at One on One in North Hollywood, with more work linked to Little Mountain in Vancouver, became famously tense. Rock challenged arrangements, tones, and performances, and Metallica pushed back.

That friction helped make *Metallica*, better known as *The Black Album*, what it is. The band stripped songs down to stronger cores. Riffs breathe more. Choruses land with real weight. Jason Newsted’s bass is actually present in the mix, which mattered after the thin low end of the previous album. On this double LP sequence, Side A opens with the near-perfect one-two punch of “Enter Sandman” and “Sad But True”, then the record keeps widening its emotional range from there.

### Heaviness through groove, space and control ⚡  
What makes this album distinctive is that it is still metal, just not metal obsessed with speed for its own sake. “Sad But True” lumbers rather than sprints, and that slower pace makes it feel huge. “The Unforgiven” and “Nothing Else Matters” bring in ballad structure, acoustic texture and a more exposed vocal style, but the emotional pull never weakens the record’s backbone. “Wherever I May Roam” adds an exotic flavour to its intro before dropping into one of the album’s grandest riffs.

The technical skill is still there, only redirected. Earlier Metallica often used complexity to create motion and tension. Here, precision serves impact. The band knows exactly when to leave space, when to repeat a riff until it becomes oppressive, and when to let James Hetfield’s voice carry the drama.

### The album that made Metallica unavoidable 📈  
Critically, the reaction at first was split. Some old-school thrash fans heard compromise. Plenty of reviewers, though, recognised that Metallica had written bigger songs without losing authority. Commercially, the result was enormous. The album hit No. 1 in the US, produced huge singles, and became one of the best-selling albums ever made. “Enter Sandman”, “The Unforgiven” and “Nothing Else Matters” moved far beyond metal radio into everyday culture.

Its political angle is less direct than some heavy records of its era. This is not a manifesto album. Still, songs full of dread, self-conflict, moral hypocrisy and hard-edged individualism fit the mood of the early 1990s, when Cold War certainties were collapsing and mass culture felt more anxious.

### Why the 2014 vinyl reissue makes sense in the streaming age 💿  
By 2014, *Metallica* had become both a canonised album and a set of songs detached into playlists, sports soundtracks and algorithm-led listening. That is where your Blackened pressing becomes interesting. It belongs to the era of streaming, social media memory, anniversary posts and endless online debate, yet it insists on the album as a physical experience. Four sides give these songs room to unfold, from “Of Wolf And Man” to “My Friend Of Misery” and “The Struggle Within”.

That tension between digital ubiquity and vinyl permanence suits *The Black Album* perfectly. It is genre-blending enough to welcome listeners from hard rock, mainstream rock and even outside metal, but heavy enough to remain unmistakably Metallica. That balance is why it still pulls in first-time listeners and lifelong fans alike.

### 🛠️ How Oasis got it on tape

*Definitely Maybe* had a more troubled birth than its swagger suggests. Oasis first tried to record it at Monnow Valley in Wales in 1993 with Dave Batchelor, but the results felt flat to the band. The songs were there, the attitude was there, yet the recordings did not hit with the force Oasis had on stage. A second attempt at Sawmills in Cornwall, with Mark Coyle closely involved, moved things nearer the target. Owen Morris then helped shape the final sound, pushing the record towards brute volume, thick guitar layers and a sense of a band charging forward in one room.

That matters because *Definitely Maybe* sounds like it is trying to burst out of the speakers. Noel Gallagher had arrived with a stockpile of songs, many written before the band were famous, and he wrote with startling certainty for a debut. On your 2014 Big Brother vinyl, the running order follows the remastered edition that restores “Sad Song” into the main sequence, after “Columbia”. That gives the album a slightly different contour from the original standard CD, where “Sad Song” was absent from the core 11 tracks.

### 🔊 The sound: loud, melodic and shamelessly direct

This is Britpop, yes, but it is also harder and rougher than that tag sometimes implies. The album pulls from punk snap, glam arrogance, Beatles-like melody and the grind of everyday northern life. “Rock ’n’ Roll Star” opens with a mission statement, while “Live Forever” turns away from grunge-era self-destruction and reaches for something more hopeful. “Columbia” and “Bring It On Down” hit with almost hypnotic force, then “Slide Away” opens into pure romantic sweep.

What makes it distinctive is how little it hides its ambitions. Oasis were happy to sound massive, familiar and catchy. There is no coyness here. Liam Gallagher’s voice has that sneering, needling quality that makes even simple lines feel loaded with attitude.

### 📈 Reception, impact and why it lasted

Released in 1994, *Definitely Maybe* went straight to number one and became the fastest-selling debut album in British chart history at the time. Critics were won over by its confidence and its run of songs, particularly “Supersonic”, “Live Forever” and “Cigarettes & Alcohol”. Over time it became one of the key records of the Britpop years, and for many listeners it remains Oasis at their purest.

Its influence runs well beyond sales. It helped make guitar bands feel central again in Britain. It also gave later indie bands permission to be tuneful, loud and openly hungry for success.

### 📱 A 1994 album in the streaming age

The 2014 reissue shows how catalogues now live in the streaming and social media world. Fans compare mixes, debate tracklists, trade favourite demos and argue over whether “Sad Song” changes the album’s mood. That kind of afterlife suits Oasis, because their story has always been larger than the music alone.

Even so, *Definitely Maybe* does not feel trapped in the 1990s. Its blend of classic rock moves, indie grit and pop choruses feels almost post-genre in hindsight. It did not reinvent rock by rejecting the past. It did it by grabbing old ideas, turning them up, and making them feel young again.

## The Bends by Radiohead 🎸

### 🎙️ From “Creep” hangover to artistic leap

By the time Radiohead began *The Bends*, they had a problem most bands would happily take, a huge hit they did not want to be trapped by. “Creep” had made them famous, but it also left them looking like a one-song grunge-era act. The sessions for *The Bends*, recorded across 1994 and early 1995 with producer John Leckie and engineer, then emerging creative partner, Nigel Godrich, became the way out.

You can hear that pressure in the record’s push and pull. Some songs were sharpened on stage before they hit tape, while others grew through layered overdubs and patient studio work at places such as RAK, The Manor and Abbey Road. Godrich’s presence matters here, because this album catches the start of one of modern rock’s great band-producer relationships. He would become central to Radiohead’s later reinventions, but the seeds are already here in the sense of space, detail and mood.

On your 2008 US Capitol vinyl pressing, that journey lands in a beautifully paced 12-track run, from the queasy lift-off of “Planet Telex” to the haunted fade of “Street Spirit (Fade Out)”. Side A builds the emotional range, Side B gets darker and tighter.

### 🎛️ What makes it sound so distinctive

*The Bends* is a guitar album, but not in a blunt, lad-rock way. The guitars shimmer, scrape, bloom and suddenly turn vicious. “Just” is all nervous energy and tangled riffs. “My Iron Lung” turns frustration into noise. “Fake Plastic Trees” begins as a fragile ballad, then swells into something almost unbearable.

Thom Yorke’s voice is just as important as the guitars. He sings like somebody trying to hold himself together in public, which gives the album its ache. That vulnerability set it apart from a lot of mid-90s rock, where swagger still carried plenty of weight. Radiohead preferred tension, uncertainty and beauty with a bruise on it.

### 📈 Reception, reputation and the road to classic status

When it came out in 1995, the album did well in Britain, reaching No. 4, but it was a slower burn in America, peaking much lower on the Billboard chart. Over time, though, its standing only grew. What first looked like a strong second album came to feel like the moment Radiohead truly became Radiohead.

Its influence runs all through later British and alternative rock. You can hear echoes of its soaring melancholy, dynamic shifts and atmospheric guitar writing in bands from Coldplay to Muse. More than that, it showed that a rock band could be emotionally open, musically ambitious and still write songs people actually wanted to live with.

### 💿 Pre-digital creation, digital-era afterlife

This was made before the file-sharing and streaming age reshaped everything, so its creation belongs to the big-studio, physical-format world. Yet its afterlife is deeply digital. Online criticism, fan culture and streaming helped turn *The Bends* from a respected 90s album into a canonised one.

As for post-9/11 or millennial themes, this is not a prophetic message board from the future. It predates all that. But its feelings of alienation, consumer disgust and emotional disconnection fit uncannily well with later anxieties. “Fake Plastic Trees” still sounds like a warning about manufactured lives, and “My Iron Lung” is a painfully sharp song about being swallowed by your own success. That ability to keep speaking to new eras is a large part of why this 2008 vinyl reissue feels so right.

## *Let’s Face It* by The Mighty Mighty Bosstones 🎺

    layout={{

### From Boston clubs to a breakthrough record 🎙️
Your 2026 US vinyl reissue brings back the Bosstones’ 1997 breakthrough in a tidy 12-track running order, and it still feels lean, fast and packed with hooks. *Let’s Face It* was the band’s fifth studio album, which matters because this was no overnight success story. By the time they made it, The Mighty Mighty Bosstones had already spent years grinding it out through touring, punk circuits and Boston’s local scene.

The album was recorded at Bearsville Sound Studios in New York and Fort Apache in Cambridge, Massachusetts, with Paul Q. Kolderie and Sean Slade producing. That pairing suits the music perfectly. Fort Apache had deep ties to US alternative rock, while the Bosstones themselves came from a scrappy, self-made scene that never lost its club-band punch. Sax player Tim Burton later said the group wanted to become a better studio band, and you can hear that discipline all over this record. The songs hit hard, but nothing feels loose or accidental.

### Ska, punk and alt-rock in one sharp package 🥁
What makes *Let’s Face It* special is how neatly it folds several worlds together. There is ska in the offbeat guitar chops, reggae in the rhythmic bounce, punk and hardcore in the speed and attack, and alternative rock in the polish and size of the choruses. The Bosstones were often filed under third-wave ska, but this album is bigger than a genre tag.

The A-side of your pressing tells that story brilliantly. “Noise Brigade” opens with instant momentum, “The Rascal King” swings with swagger, and “Royal Oil” leans into the band’s ska roots before “The Impression That I Get” lands as the undeniable centrepiece. Even the title track and “Another Drinkin’ Song” keep that mix of bite and singalong lift.

A big part of the sound is the rhythm section. The guitar rarely behaves like grunge-era sludge. Instead, it jabs, chops and drives. The bass gives the songs weight without clogging them up, and the drums push everything forward with a firm rock backbeat. Then the horns arrive, not as decoration, but as part of the engine.

### A different answer to the grunge decade 📻
By 1997, the alternative boom had opened up far beyond grunge. *Let’s Face It* arrived at exactly the right moment. Where so much early 90s rock was inward, muddy or brooding, the Bosstones sounded communal, rhythmic and streetwise. They offered movement instead of drag, brass instead of guitar heroics, and choruses built for packed rooms.

That shift helped the album connect well beyond ska fans. “The Impression That I Get” went to No. 1 on Billboard’s Modern Rock chart, “The Rascal King” also became a major modern-rock hit, and the album reached No. 27 on the Billboard 200 before going platinum in the US.

### Why it still matters 💥
This album’s legacy is larger than one massive single. *Let’s Face It* proved that a horn-driven band with deep DIY roots could cross into the mainstream without sanding off its identity. It gave third-wave ska one of its defining records, and it still sounds like a reminder that 90s alternative music was far more musically open than the grunge shorthand suggests.

Flip to side B and that point holds. “Numbered Days”, “Break So Easily” and “Desensitized” keep the pace up, while “1-2-8” closes with the band’s mix of toughness and bounce intact. It is a compact album, but that economy is part of its charm. There is no filler here, just a seasoned band hitting its mark.

## *We Are Unpeople* by Unpeople 🎸

### From two guitars to a full debut 🔧
*We Are Unpeople* arrived on SharpTone on 9 October 2026, and it feels like the sound of a band turning early chemistry into a proper statement. In interviews around the release, guitarist Luke Caley described Unpeople’s beginnings in very direct terms: he and vocalist Jake Crawford started writing together with two guitars, without trying to force the project into a neat genre box. That matters, because this album has the feel of something built from instinct first, branding second.

Caley also spoke about the record as a continuation of the band’s earlier EP rather than a hard reset. You can hear that in the way these songs feel sharpened rather than reinvented. One lovely detail from the album’s making is that “bottle it” was played live before it was fully finished, and became the first song they tackled in the album sessions. That suggests a band using the stage as a workshop, testing weight, pacing and hooks in front of real people before committing them to tape.

### Heavy, melodic, and happily hard to pin down 🌩️
This is a modern rock record, but that label only gets you so far. The album moves through alternative metal, alternative rock and hard rock with a lot of ease. The running order on your vinyl makes that clear. Side A is punchy and immediate, with songs like “clouds”, “friends” and “swallow” landing quickly and hard. Side B opens things out a bit more, with “haunted” and the closing “as you were” giving the band room to stretch emotionally.

What makes Unpeople distinctive is how natural that movement feels. “blueprints” was discussed by the band as a more ballad-like piece, yet it does not sound like a token softer moment dropped into a heavy album for variety’s sake.
