### 🎙️ Built in fragments, finished under strain

Your 2016 European Pink Floyd Records vinyl reissue preserves an album that first arrived in 1979 as a double LP, and it still feels made for four sides of vinyl. Side A moves from “In The Flesh?” to “Mother” like the first act of a stage work. Side B deepens Pink’s isolation, Side C opens the wall from the inside, and Side D turns ugly, theatrical and finally rueful with “The Trial” and “Outside The Wall”.

The idea came from Roger Waters after Pink Floyd’s 1977 tour, when he felt cut off from the huge crowds in front of him. He imagined that emotional barrier as a literal wall, built brick by brick from war trauma, school cruelty, fame, sex, fear and loneliness. Recording stretched across Britain, France, New York and Los Angeles, with Bob Ezrin helping shape Waters’ sprawling demos into a tighter rock opera. The making of the album was difficult. Richard Wright’s place in the band was weakening, tempers were short, and the sessions have become almost as famous as the music. You can hear the ambition in the finished record, but also the pressure behind it.

### 🎸 The sound, from disco pulse to operatic drama

What makes *The Wall* distinctive is how often it changes shape without losing its thread. It is prog rock, art rock and classic rock, but it also borrows from theatrical writing, film sound design and even disco. “Another Brick In The Wall Part 2” has that clipped groove and dance-floor pulse, which is part of why it became such a huge single. Elsewhere, the album shifts from the acoustic unease of “Mother” to the heavy swagger of “Young Lust”, then into the haunted hush of “Hey You”.

David Gilmour’s guitar gives the record much of its emotional lift. “Comfortably Numb” is the obvious peak, with solos that sing rather than shred, but his playing across the album is just as important. On “Run Like Hell” he turns rhythm into menace, while the harsher riffs of “In The Flesh” add weight to the story’s fascist nightmare. Nick Mason’s drumming is never flashy, yet it keeps the songs moving with a disciplined feel, and Waters’ bass lines do the same job, steadying the whole structure.

### 📰 Hit album, divided reviews, huge afterlife

When *The Wall* came out, it sold enormously, even if some reviewers found it bleak or overblown. Time has been kinder. “Another Brick In The Wall Part 2” became a worldwide hit, and the album is now treated as one of Pink Floyd’s defining works.

Its legacy reaches far beyond classic rock. Later concept albums, arena productions and politically charged rock records owe it something. In the streaming era, songs like “Comfortably Numb” and “Another Brick In The Wall Part 2” live far beyond the album format, but social media has also kept the bigger themes in play. Its attacks on authoritarian schooling, mass manipulation and emotional isolation still feel uncomfortably current. For a record born at the end of the 1970s, *The Wall* remains very easy to recognise in modern life.

### A definitive map of The Police story 🗺️

This 2023 European 2LP edition of **Greatest Hits** brings together the 1992 compilation in a handsome vinyl form, released by **Polydor and UMC** and remastered at **Abbey Road**. Your pressing keeps the original logic of that earlier set intact: it is less a random pile of famous songs than a neat arc through the band’s rise from 1978 to 1983. Side A starts in the nervy, lean world of *Roxanne* and *Can’t Stand Losing You*. By Side D, you are deep into the polished, haunted grandeur of *Every Breath You Take*, *King of Pain* and *Tea in the Sahara*.

That running order matters. It lets you hear The Police growing from a wiry trio with punk energy and reggae instincts into a huge pop-rock force with darker moods and more spacious production. The inclusion of **The Bed’s Too Big Without You** and **Tea in the Sahara** is a lovely touch, because they remind you that the band’s best moments were not only the obvious singles.

### How these songs were made 🎚️

Because this is a compilation, its “creation process” stretches across five studio albums. The early material was largely produced by **Nigel Gray**, whose recordings caught the band in a taut, hungry form. You can hear that economy in *So Lonely* and *Message in a Bottle*, where Andy Summers’ clipped guitar, Sting’s melodic bass, and Stewart Copeland’s restless drumming leave a huge amount of space without sounding thin.

Later tracks move into the **Hugh Padgham** era, especially on *Ghost in the Machine* and *Synchronicity*. That is where the sound becomes broader and more atmospheric. *Every Little Thing She Does Is Magic* has a richer, brighter pop sheen, while *Invisible Sun* and *Spirits in the Material World* bring in a colder, more political tone. *Every Breath You Take* sounds pristine, but its calm surface hides a song about obsession, not romance.

As for the pandemic question, it did **not** affect the making of the music itself. These recordings are vintage work from 1978 to 1983. The 2023 edition is a remastering and reissue project, not a remotely assembled new album.

### Why it still sounds fresh 🌍

What makes The Police distinctive is how casually they bend genre. This is rock, yes, but it is rock full of **reggae pulse, dub space, pop hooks and post-punk tension**. They challenged rock conventions by refusing to sound heavy in the usual sense. Instead, they used rhythm, silence and precision.

That fluidity feels very current. In a musical culture where genre lines are constantly blurred, The Police sound oddly modern. Songs such as *De Do Do Do, De Da Da Da* also land differently now, with their sharp view of empty public language and slogan-driven communication.

### Legacy, hits, and darker themes 👀

Commercially, these songs were enormous, with a run of UK number ones that made The Police one of the defining bands of their era. Critically, their reputation lasts because the hits carry real tension. *Don’t Stand So Close to Me* deals with abuse of power. *Invisible Sun* draws from political conflict. *Synchronicity II* turns suburban dread into something almost mythic.

So this record is not just a greatest-hits package. It is a portrait of a band that made clever, uneasy, rhythm-rich music feel like mainstream pop. That is why it still pulls listeners in.

### 🌌 Recording it in New York, aiming for outer space

Originally released in 1995, *Dopes To Infinity* caught Monster Magnet at the point where their heavy riff attack grew more ambitious and more colourful. Dave Wyndorf led the sessions in New York, mainly at The Magic Shop, with some work at Electric Lady, and he also produced the record himself. That matters, because this album feels less like a band bashing out songs and more like a very specific headspace built in the studio.

There was strain behind the scenes too. Bass duties were less straightforward than on paper, with Wyndorf stepping in on bass as well, and that gave the record an unusually controlled, authored feel. The sound widened beyond brute-force stoner rock through mellotron, organ and sitar, while Alan Moulder’s mixing helped turn the whole thing into a dense, swirling rush rather than a plain hard rock set.

Your 2016 European vinyl edition makes that expansiveness easy to hear, especially across four sides. The bonus material on side D, “Forbidden Planet”, “Eclipse This” and a cover of “I’m Five Years Ahead Of My Time”, also shows how naturally Monster Magnet’s own songs sat beside garage-psych and sci-fi camp.

### 🎸 The riffs, the groove, the cosmic swagger

What makes *Dopes To Infinity* distinctive is the way it joins hard rock muscle to psychedelic drift. Ed Mundell’s guitar work is central. “Negasonic Teenage Warhead” has that flanged, needling riff that sounds both catchy and slightly unhinged, while tracks like “Look To Your Orb For The Warning” and “King Of Mars” lean into a huge, druggy churn.

The rhythm section keeps all this from floating away. Jon Kleiman’s drumming is direct and weighty, with a proper rock pulse rather than a loose jam-band feel, and the bass sits thick in the mix, often locking to the guitar until the whole song seems to roll forward on one giant wheel. That push-pull between trance and drive is the album’s real magic.

### 📈 Reception, reputation and long afterlife

Back in 1995, the album was well reviewed and even picked up praise from mainstream outlets such as *Entertainment Weekly*, but sales were solid rather than massive. It charted respectably in Europe and on the US Heatseekers chart, yet it never became a full crossover smash.

Its reputation has only improved. For many listeners, this is one of Monster Magnet’s key records, the point where space rock, Sabbath-sized riffing, garage psych and 90s alternative songcraft all clicked. You can hear its shadow in later stoner and psych-heavy bands that wanted more than fuzz and volume, they wanted atmosphere too.

### 📱 Why it still makes sense in the streaming era

Even though this is a deeply mid-90s album, it travels well in the streaming age. Modern listeners jump easily between heavy psych, alt-metal, classic hard rock and space rock, and *Dopes To Infinity* fits all those lanes without sounding confused. That kind of genre blur feels very current.

It is not a political album in any direct sense, but its retreat into sci-fi imagery, cosmic jokes and altered-state excess gave 1995 an escape route from dour post-grunge realism. In the 2010s vinyl revival, and on streaming playlists now, that escape still feels good. This record has weight, weirdness and hooks, and it still sounds gloriously out of step.

## Siamese Dream by The Smashing Pumpkins 🎸

    layout={{

### Recording under pressure, then building a wall of sound 🛠️

Even on your 2011 US Virgin 2LP reissue, *Siamese Dream* still feels like a record made in a fever. The original album arrived in 1993, after *Gish* had given the band a foothold and after *Nevermind* had changed the stakes for every American guitar group in sight. Billy Corgan and producer Butch Vig took the band to Los Angeles and made an album that sounded huge, exacting and, at times, slightly haunted.

The sessions were famously tense. Corgan has spoken about perfectionism, depression and the pressure around the band at the time, and that strain fed directly into the music. A lot of the album’s sound came from obsessive overdubbing, with layers upon layers of guitars stacked until the songs felt almost orchestral. That matters when you drop the needle on this pressing: “Cherub Rock”, “Hummer” and “Soma” are not simply loud, they are carefully built. Jimmy Chamberlin’s drumming is a big reason the album never turns muddy, because his playing keeps all that density moving.

### Sweetness, noise and strange beauty 🌙

What makes *Siamese Dream* special is how many different rock languages it speaks at once. There is grunge in the weight of the guitars, shoegaze in the blur and shimmer, metal in the attack, and a bit of prog in the long builds and dramatic turns. Yet it never feels like a stitched-together exercise. It sounds like one band chasing one emotional world.

The running order on your copy makes that clear. Side A opens with force, then “Today” slips in with that bright, unforgettable riff wrapped around deeply bleak lyrics. Side B brings “Disarm”, with strings and acoustic guitar, sounding almost fragile beside “Rocket” and “Soma”. By the time Side C gets to “Mayonaise” and “Spaceboy”, the album is at its most open-hearted. Then Side D closes with the sprawl of “Silverfuck” before the hush of “Sweet Sweet” and the soft landing of “Luna”. It is sequenced like a release of pressure.

### Reception, success and why it lasted 📻

At the time, critics in both the US and UK were struck by how ambitious it was. *Rolling Stone* praised its growth from *Gish*, and the album became the band’s commercial breakthrough, helped by heavy MTV and radio play for “Cherub Rock”, “Today” and “Disarm”. It pushed The Smashing Pumpkins out of cult territory and into the centre of 1990s alternative rock.

Its legacy has only grown. A lot of later emo, alt-rock and shoegaze revival bands borrowed from this record’s mix of heaviness and vulnerability. “Mayonaise” and “Soma” in particular have become the sort of songs that younger listeners keep rediscovering and passing around online.

### From the 1990s to streaming-era afterlife 📱

Although *Siamese Dream* belongs to the early 1990s, its afterlife is thoroughly modern. Streaming has given the album a second, third and fourth life, and social media keeps deep cuts circulating well beyond the old singles. It also sounds oddly current now. In an age where genre lines are loose and production can be maximal, this album feels less like a period piece and more like an early example of post-genre rock thinking big.

It also pushed against the old rock idea that authenticity had to mean rawness. *Siamese Dream* is polished, layered and studio-crafted, but none of that makes it emotionally distant. If anything, the artifice lets the feeling hit harder. That is why this 2011 vinyl reissue still lands so well: the album is massive, wounded and meticulously made, all at once.

## Devil Hopping by Inspiral Carpets 🚀

### Recording a band, not a studio puzzle 🎛️

Released in the UK on 7 March 1994 through Mute and Cow, *Devil Hopping* caught Inspiral Carpets at an interesting point. This was their fourth studio album, and they wanted it to feel more like a proper group performance than a polished assembly job. The record was made at Parr Street Studios in Liverpool with Pascal Gabriel producing, alongside the band, and Clif Norrell engineering and mixing.

Clint Boon later explained that the band had grown tired of the stop-start way records could be made, with members dropping in separately and technology tidying up the rough edges. For *Devil Hopping*, they rehearsed hard beforehand and then worked together in the studio, aiming for more spontaneity. There is a lovely human detail in that choice too, wives and children were around during the sessions, which gives the album a communal, grounded feel rather than one of rock-star distance. Even the title came from Gabriel’s pronunciation of “developing”, which sounded like “devil hopping” to the band.

### Garage-psych in the age of grunge 👁️

What makes *Devil Hopping* distinctive is that it does not try to become grunge, even though it arrived in 1994 when grunge still loomed large. Inspiral Carpets instead leaned back into what they did best: wiry guitars, Farfisa-style organ, sharp rhythms, and that mix of garage rock punch with psychedelic colour. It has the snap of indie rock, but also a slightly strange, cartoonish quality that always made the band feel a bit sideways from their peers.

Your vinyl sequence works well in that respect. Side A opens with “I Want You” and keeps things moving through “Party In The Sky”, “Plutoman” and “Uniform”, while Side B lands the big hit “Saturn 5” early, then heads into moodier territory with “The Way The Lights Fall” and the brief sting of “Cobra”. “Saturn 5” is the album’s clearest mission statement, all propulsion and oddball space-race imagery.

### Hits, reviews and Mark E. Smith’s ghostly visit 📻

Commercially, the album did well enough, reaching No. 10 on the UK Albums Chart. “Saturn 5” reached No. 20, while “I Want You” climbed even higher to No. 18 in its single form featuring Mark E. Smith of The Fall. That collaboration is one of the album’s best stories. Smith came in, delivered a pile of wild ad-libs and vocal takes, and Norrell pieced them together into something jagged and memorable.

Reviews were mixed. Some writers liked the tougher return to garage-psych basics, while others thought the band had lost some of their earlier spark. Even so, the singles have lasted better than some of the reviews.

### Why *Devil Hopping* still matters 🌌

Its legacy is less about changing rock history than proving there was another path through the 1990s. Inspiral Carpets answered the decade’s musical sprawl, grunge, Britpop, post-punk aftershocks, by sounding more like themselves. That in itself feels quite stubborn and very indie. *Devil Hopping* is the sound of a band refusing to chase fashion, and there is real charm in that. It was also their last studio album before the first split, which gives it the feel of a closing statement, scruffy, catchy, strange, and unmistakably theirs.

## Pablo Honey by Radiohead 🎸

### 🛠️ How it was made

Radiohead recorded *Pablo Honey* in 1992, mainly at Chipping Norton Recording Studios in Oxfordshire, with Sean Slade and Paul Q. Kolderie producing. That pairing mattered. Slade and Kolderie had worked with American alternative bands such as Pixies and Dinosaur Jr, so the album arrived with a rougher US college-rock bite than many British guitar records of the time. The sessions were fairly quick, about three weeks of concentrated recording, tracked in a traditional studio set-up rather than the more obsessive, open-ended methods the band would use later.

You can hear a young group still working out what kind of band it wants to be. Thom Yorke’s voice is already distinctive, but the record is less about studio trickery than direct performance. Even the most famous moment came from instinct rather than grand design: Jonny Greenwood’s violent guitar stabs in “Creep” were reportedly his way of disrupting a song he thought sounded too pretty. Instead, they became the hook that helped send the track round the world.

### 🎧 The sound of early Radiohead

This is a guitar album through and through. On your 2016 XL vinyl reissue, the sequence still feels like a compact statement of early-90s alternative rock, from the surge of “You” through to the hazy, stronger-than-people-remember “Blow Out”. Side A leans into immediacy, with “Creep”, “Stop Whispering” and “Anyone Can Play Guitar” giving the record its clearest singles shape. Side B is scrappier and moodier, which suits the album well.

What makes *Pablo Honey* interesting is not that it sounds like later Radiohead, because mostly it does not. It draws from Pixies-style quiet-loud dynamics, grunge-era tension, indie jangle and straightforward rock structures. Yet there are hints of the band they would become. “Blow Out” stretches into a more atmospheric space, and Yorke already writes from a place of unease, awkwardness and self-disgust that cut deeper than standard indie-rock bravado.

### 📈 Reception, then and now

When it came out in 1993, the response was mixed to positive. Plenty of reviewers liked “Creep” but found the album itself uneven or too close to its influences. Commercially, though, “Creep” changed everything. After a slow start, it became a major hit, especially once US radio and MTV picked it up.

That success was a mixed blessing. For years, *Pablo Honey* was treated as the lesser Radiohead album, the one before the real story began. Later records, especially *The Bends* and *OK Computer*, made its limits plain. Still, distance has been kind to it. Heard now, it is a sharp snapshot of a band at the beginning rather than a failed draft.

### 🌍 Legacy in the streaming era

The 2016 XL reissue gave *Pablo Honey* a fresh life on vinyl just as streaming culture made full-catalogue listening normal. Younger fans could jump from *A Moon Shaped Pool* straight back to “You” and hear the contrast in seconds. That has helped the album’s reputation. It no longer has to compete with later masterpieces on impossible terms. Instead, it works as an origin point.

It is not a political album, and it does not smash rock conventions in the way Radiohead later would. What it does capture is the early-90s mood of alienation and uncertainty, with “Creep” turning self-loathing into a huge communal singalong. That alone gave rock a different emotional register, and it still lands.

## *In The Aeroplane Over The Sea* by Neutral Milk Hotel ✈️

<LightGallery
    layout={{

### 🎙️ A fast, homemade recording with strange magic
Released in the US on 10 February 1998 by Merge Records, *In The Aeroplane Over The Sea* came together at Pet Sounds Studio in Denver with Robert Schneider producing. Jeff Mangum arrived with songs that already felt vivid and unsettling, and the sessions moved quickly, which suits the album’s character. It has the feeling of something caught before it could be polished smooth.

That directness matters. Schneider’s production leans into compression, tape warmth and a rough edge rather than tidying everything into late-90s studio gloss. Even the longest and most emotionally draining piece, “Oh Comely”, has become part of the album’s lore because Mangum reportedly delivered it in a single take. You can hear why people fixate on that. The performance sounds less like a studio construction than a confession that happened to be recorded.

The sequencing on your CD helps the record land as a single arc. “The King Of Carrot Flowers Pt. One” and “Pts. Two & Three” open like a doorway into Mangum’s private mythology, and “Two-Headed Boy Pt. Two” closes the whole thing with a haunted, almost emptied-out grace.

### 🥁 Folk-punk, brass bands and a rhythm section that pushes everything forward
People often file this under indie rock, but that barely covers it. This album pulls together acoustic folk strumming, fuzzy electric guitar, brass fanfares, drones, organ and a drum sound that often feels more like a parade or processional than a standard rock backbeat. It is lo-fi, but not in a careless way. The arrangements are packed, yet the songs still feel bare-nerved.

The guitar work is a big part of that tension. Mangum’s acoustic playing is insistent and percussive, almost battering at times, while the electric parts arrive in bursts of distortion that sound ecstatic rather than sleek. The rhythm section does not swing in a relaxed way. It thumps, marches and surges. Listen to “Holland, 1945”, where the drums and bass give the song its breathless charge, or the title track, where the strumming turns simple chords into something overwhelming.

### 🌍 A different answer to the 90s than grunge gave
By 1998, the first wave of grunge had already changed rock, but Neutral Milk Hotel took a different route through the decade. Instead of hard-edged alienation or major-label heft, this album came from the Elephant 6 world, a loose indie community where homemade methods, odd sounds and shared imagination counted for more than polish.

That made *In The Aeroplane Over The Sea* feel separate from mainstream alternative rock while still belonging to the same decade’s explosion of possibilities. It answered the 90s with intimacy, surreal imagery and a sound that welcomed folk, psychedelia and punk energy into the same room.

### 🌟 From modest release to cult canon
Its first reception was good rather than rapturous, and sales were modest at the start. Over time, though, the album grew by word of mouth into one of the defining indie records of the 1990s. Its influence lives in the way later artists embraced fragile vocals, homemade textures and emotional openness without sanding off the weird bits.

Few records from that era have had such a dramatic second life. This one began as an eccentric Merge CD and ended up as a touchstone.

## Slayed? by Slade 🎸

<LightGallery
    layout={{
        imgs: [
            { src: "/assets/2026-09-14-listened-to-this-week/albums/Slayed.jpg", alt: "Slayed? by Slade" },
            { src: "/assets/2026-09-14-listened-to-this-week/artists/Slade.jpg", alt: "Slade" }
        ]
    }}
/>


### Recording it hot and fast 🔥

Released in the UK on 1 December 1972, *Slayed?* caught Slade at the moment when their studio records finally matched the mayhem of their live reputation. Producer Chas Chandler, already central to the band’s rise, understood that Slade did not need tidying up so much as focusing. The trick was to bottle that rowdy, sweat-soaked stage energy without draining the grit away.

That is all over this LP. The songs are short, direct and built to land quickly. Holder and bassist Jim Lea wrote most of the material, and they wrote like men who knew exactly how a crowd would react. Even the sequencing on your UK Polydor pressing feels purposeful: side one charges in with “How D’You Ride” and “The Whole World’s Goin’ Crazee”, then side two opens with the knockout punch of “Gudbuy T’ Jane” before driving towards “Mama Weer All Crazee Now”. It is an album arranged for impact.

### The sound, stomp and spelling ✨

What makes *Slayed?* distinctive is how neatly it joins hard rock muscle with pop instinct. Slade were filed under glam, and the glam tag fits, but this is not all glitter and pose. There is boogie in it, pub-rock punch in it, and a big communal singalong spirit in nearly every chorus.

The famously misspelt titles help, of course. “Gudbuy T’ Jane” and “Mama Weer All Crazee Now” look cheeky on the sleeve, but they also tell you something about the band. Slade wanted to sound like ordinary British voices turned up to full volume. Noddy Holder’s bark, half shout and half grin, gave the songs their rough charm.

Dave Hill’s guitar work is central here. He is not trying to be a flashy virtuoso. The riffs jab, churn and push. Jim Lea’s bass keeps everything bouncing, while Don Powell’s drumming gives the record its stomp. That rhythm section is a huge part of why *Slayed?* feels physical, almost like you can hear boots hitting a wooden floor.

### Hits, reviews and chart power 📈

The album was a major success. It hit No. 1 in the UK and stayed on the chart for months, with strong runs elsewhere too, including the US, where it became Slade’s biggest album of the decade. That matters because Slade were often seen first as a singles band, yet *Slayed?* proved they could make a proper hit LP as well.

Reviews at the time were strong. *NME* raved about it, and other papers praised its stomping force. That reaction makes sense, because this is one of those records where the singles do not flatter weak album tracks. “Look At Last Nite”, “I Don’ Mind” and the rollicking “Let The Good Times Roll” help give it shape beyond the obvious crowd-pleasers.

### Legacy and the digital afterlife 💿

*Slayed?* remains one of the defining glam rock albums, and for many fans it is Slade’s best studio statement. You can hear its echo in later hard rock and party rock, especially in bands that favour chantable choruses over fancy playing.
