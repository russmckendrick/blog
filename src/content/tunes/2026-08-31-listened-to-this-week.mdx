### A career in one box 📀

*Forty Licks* began life in 2002 as the Stones’ 40th anniversary compilation, and that matters because it was more than a routine greatest-hits set. For years, the band’s 1960s recordings and their post-1970 work sat in different rights camps, with ABKCO controlling the early catalogue and the later material tied to the band’s own business arrangements. This release finally pulled both eras together in one package, which made it feel like the first truly complete Rolling Stones overview.

Your vinyl edition turns that CD-era anthology into a large-scale eight-side journey, and the running order really suits that role. The early sides move through the scrappy R&B roots, the flash of 60s singles, and the darker late-60s classics. By the time the set reaches “Start Me Up”, “Miss You”, “Beast Of Burden” and “Love Is Strong”, you can hear the band ageing without losing its swagger. It is a compilation, but it plays like a story.

### Old hits, new songs, same attitude 🔥

The distinctive twist in *Forty Licks* was the addition of four new studio tracks, “Don’t Stop”, “Key To Your Love”, “Stealing My Heart” and “Losing My Touch”. Those songs were recorded in Paris in 2002 with Don Was producing, Chuck Leavell on keyboards and Darryl Jones on bass. They were there to give long-time fans a reason to buy the album, but they also act as a snapshot of what the Stones sounded like in the early 2000s.

Musically, the whole set covers blues rock, rock ’n’ roll, country-leaning balladry, disco-inflected groove and hard-edged riff rock. What keeps it coherent is the band’s feel. Keith Richards’ guitar work is the spine of the collection, from the blunt attack of “Satisfaction” and “Jumpin’ Jack Flash” to the open-tuned chug of “Start Me Up”. Ronnie Wood’s weaving rhythm style on the later cuts gives the songs that loose, slightly dangerous shape the Stones do better than almost anyone.

### Charlie Watts is the secret weapon 🥁

Across four decades of material, Charlie Watts is the great constant. His drumming never begs for attention, but it gives the Stones their balance, swing and dry punch. Bill Wyman’s bass on the classic tracks often creeps around the beat in clever little ways, while the later songs with Darryl Jones feel cleaner and more modern. That shift is part of why *Forty Licks* works so well as a long listen, you hear the rhythm section change with the times while the band’s identity stays intact.

### Reception, legacy and the digital age 🌍

Critics generally liked *Forty Licks* as a practical, generous overview, even if every fan can argue about omissions. Commercially, it arrived at the right moment, tied to the 40 Licks tour and aimed at a market that still bought major catalogue CDs in huge numbers. In the streaming era, its importance has grown again because it functions almost like an official master playlist.

There is also something quietly millennial about it. Released in the shadow of post-9/11 uncertainty, *Forty Licks* leans into memory, continuity and survival. “Losing My Touch” in particular has an older, more reflective mood. This is not the sound of a band pretending it is still 1965. It is the sound of a band taking stock, and still landing the punch.

### From break-ups to blockbuster pop 📀
Although your copy is a **2014 European vinyl pressing**, *Greatest Hits* itself began life in **1988** as a smart summary of Fleetwood Mac’s Warner Bros. peak. It gathers tracks recorded between **1975 and 1988**, so this is less a single-session album than a map of the Buckingham-Nicks-McVie era at full strength. The running order on your pressing makes that clear straight away: side A moves from the mystical sweep of **“Rhiannon”** into the brisk optimism of **“Don’t Stop”**, then the clipped drive of **“Go Your Own Way”**, before moving through the sleek 1980s sheen of **“Hold Me”** and **“Everywhere”**.

That spread matters. These songs came from very different sessions and moods, from the polished California studio craft of *Fleetwood Mac* and *Rumours* to the stranger, more restless world of *Tusk*, then on to the immaculate pop surface of *Mirage* and *Tango in the Night*. ### What makes the sound so addictive 🎸
Fleetwood Mac’s gift was turning emotional chaos into elegant pop-rock. This compilation catches that brilliantly. Lindsey Buckingham’s guitar playing is a huge part of it: sharp, percussive, often more about rhythm and texture than flashy solos. Listen to **“Go Your Own Way”** or **“Tusk”** and you can hear how much motion he creates with attack alone.

Underneath him, **John McVie** and **Mick Fleetwood** keep everything grounded. McVie’s bass lines are melodic without getting fussy, while Fleetwood’s drumming has a roomy, almost conversational feel. Together they give songs like **“Dreams”**, **“Sara”** and **“Little Lies”** their steady pull. Add the vocal contrast of Stevie Nicks, Christine McVie and Buckingham, and the set becomes a lesson in how rock, pop, soft rock and a hint of new wave could live happily in the same band.

### Reception, afterlife and the streaming age 📈
Commercially, *Greatest Hits* was a monster. It became one of Fleetwood Mac’s longest-lasting catalogue titles, charted strongly on both sides of the Atlantic, and went multi-platinum. That makes sense, because it works almost like the ideal starter pack: no filler, just songs people already half-know before the chorus lands.

In the streaming era, that hit-packed design has aged extremely well. Modern listeners often meet Fleetwood Mac one song at a time, through playlists, TikTok clips, film soundtracks or a recommendation after hearing **“Dreams”** for the hundredth time. This compilation fits that listening habit neatly, even though it was built decades earlier.

It was assembled like a smart, label-savvy shop stereo playlist, designed to sell the mood of the film and move records in its own right.

That strategy worked better than the film’s first cinema run did. The movie underperformed at the box office, but the soundtrack took on a life of its own, helped in no small part by Gin Blossoms’ “Til I Hear It From You”, which became one of the defining radio songs attached to any 90s soundtrack. ### A new line-up, a new sound 🎸
Released originally in 1979, *Drums And Wires* caught XTC at exactly the right moment of reinvention. Keyboard player Barry Andrews had gone, and in came guitarist Dave Gregory, whose arrival changed the group’s balance completely. Instead of the twitchy organ-heavy attack of the early records, XTC became a lean four-piece built around Terry Chambers’ drums and two sharply contrasting guitars. Andy Partridge could now write more texturally, while Gregory brought polish, melodic sense and a very English kind of musical precision.

The album was recorded at The Town House in London in June and July 1979, produced by Steve Lillywhite and engineered by Hugh Padgham. That pairing matters. They gave the record its hard, roomy drum sound and its bright, cutting guitar lines. The title is almost a mission statement. You hear the drums up front, and the wires are the guitars, taut, electric and always in motion.

### Post-punk brains with pop nerve 📻
What makes *Drums And Wires* so distinctive is how it joins post-punk tension to pop craft. Songs jerk and twist, but they never feel abstract for the sake of it. “Making Plans For Nigel” is the obvious centrepiece, with its mechanical pulse, clipped vocal delivery and sly portrait of a life planned by other people. Colin Moulding wrote it as a song about parental control and workaday conformity, and it still lands hard in an age of managed careers, burnout and corporate cheerfulness.

Elsewhere, “Roads Girdle The Globe” turns Partridge’s dislike of car culture into something frantic and physical, a theme that now feels uncannily current with all our arguments about climate and infrastructure. “Complicated Game” closes the album in almost frightening fashion, building from unease into a near-shouted collapse. That art-school instinct, clever but never bloodless, runs all through the record.

### Reception, hits and long afterlife 🌟
This was the album that pushed XTC into wider view. “Making Plans For Nigel” became their breakthrough single, reaching No. 17 in the UK, and the album itself is often treated as the point where the band truly became XTC in the fullest sense. Critics responded to its sharp writing and fresh sound, even if its commercial impact was modest beside the biggest names of the era.

Its influence has lasted because it catches several things at once: post-punk restlessness, new wave clarity, pop hooks, and studio craft that pointed towards the 1980s. Padgham’s drum treatment in particular feels like an early sign of a production style that would soon be everywhere.

### A retreat that changed the record 🏡
Your 2014 European Atlantic vinyl reissue preserves one of Led Zeppelin’s most surprising turns. Originally released in 1970, *Led Zeppelin III* grew out of a pause in the band’s relentless touring. Jimmy Page and Robert Plant spent time at Bron-Yr-Aur, a remote cottage in Wales with no electricity, and that break from the road fed the album’s more rustic side. You can hear it in the running order on this pressing, where the blast of “Immigrant Song” gives way to the strange, winding “Friends”, then later opens into the gentler world of “Tangerine”, “That’s The Way” and “Bron-Y-Aur Stomp”.

Much of the album was then recorded at Headley Grange with the Rolling Stones Mobile Studio, with further work at Olympic and Island in London. That mix of rural writing and flexible recording spaces mattered. The band could keep the looseness of ideas first sketched on acoustic guitars, then hit hard when they wanted. “Since I’ve Been Loving You” is the clearest example, a slow blues that feels lived-in rather than polished.

### Folk, blues and hard rock in the same breath 🎸
What makes *Led Zeppelin III* distinctive is how boldly it shifts between attack and intimacy. Zeppelin had already built a reputation on force, but here they lean into British and American folk, country touches, modal acoustic writing and old blues forms without sounding like tourists in someone else’s style.

Page’s guitar work is the key. He moves from the clipped, urgent riff of “Immigrant Song” to delicate acoustic picking on “Tangerine” and the brisk strum-and-run energy of “Bron-Y-Aur Stomp”. John Paul Jones adds colour everywhere, from bass and arrangement detail to the unsettled feel of “Friends”. John Bonham, meanwhile, keeps the album from drifting into prettiness. Even on the acoustic material, his drumming has weight and motion. On the heavier cuts, he and Jones give the songs their punch and swing rather than mere volume.

### A confused first reaction, then a warmer verdict 📻
At the time, some critics and fans were thrown by the acoustic emphasis after *Led Zeppelin II*. Commercially, that hardly hurt it. The album sold strongly and topped charts, but its reputation took time to settle. In hindsight, many listeners hear it as the record where Zeppelin proved they were far more than a heavy riff machine.

That reassessment has lasted because the album feels adventurous without sounding self-conscious. “Gallows Pole” reshapes a traditional song into something tense and accelerating, and “Hats Off To (Roy) Harper” ends the LP in a woozy blues haze that still feels odd today.

### Legacy, and how it lives now 💿
This album’s long shadow lies in its freedom. It gave later rock bands permission to move between acoustic folk textures and hard electric force on the same record. It also fits modern listening in an interesting way. In the streaming era, “Immigrant Song” often travels as the big standalone anthem, but the full album tells a different story, one about contrast, sequencing and mood. That is part of why the 2014 vinyl reissue makes sense. *Led Zeppelin III* is an album that rewards side-by-side listening, from A1 to B5, where its turns feel deliberate rather than abrupt.

It is not a political record, but it does carry that early-1970s back-to-the-land mood, a desire to step away from noise and find something older, stranger and more human.
