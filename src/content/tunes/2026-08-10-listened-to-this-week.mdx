### A difficult birth, and a late-80s comeback 🎛️
Released in April 1987, *Tango in the Night* had a long and fraught journey to the finish line. It began as a Lindsey Buckingham solo project, then slowly turned into a Fleetwood Mac record during sessions that ran from late 1985 into early 1987 at Rumbo Recorders and Buckingham’s home studio, The Slope, in Los Angeles. That origin matters, because the album often feels like Fleetwood Mac filtered through Buckingham’s private laboratory.

He and co-producer Richard Dashut built tracks piece by piece, layering parts over months and polishing arrangements with almost obsessive care. Some mixes reportedly took days, even a week, to finish. Stevie Nicks was not deeply involved in the sessions for much of that period, partly because of personal struggles, yet her voice and songs still give the record part of its mystique. It was also the last studio album by the band’s classic five-member line-up, which gives it an extra charge in hindsight.

### Glossy pop, strange shadows, and that Buckingham touch ✨
What makes *Tango in the Night* so distinctive is the way it joins Fleetwood Mac’s melodic gift to 1980s studio sheen. This is a rock album, but it lives comfortably with synth-pop and new-wave ideas. The keyboards gleam, the vocals are stacked into silky choirs, and the drums have that clean, gated 80s snap. Yet it never sounds like the band simply chasing fashion.

Buckingham’s guitar work is a huge part of that balance. On “Big Love”, the guitar is not just decoration, it is the pulse, all clipped attack and wiry tension. Across the album, guitars often act like rhythmic textures, tucked into the production alongside synths rather than sitting above them in a traditional rock mix. Underneath all that gloss, Mick Fleetwood and John McVie keep the songs grounded. Fleetwood’s drumming has space and character, while McVie’s bass gives warmth to tracks that might otherwise feel too sleek.

### MTV-era Fleetwood Mac 📺
This album arrived at exactly the right moment for the visual pop world. Songs like “Big Love”, “Little Lies”, “Everywhere”, and “Seven Wonders” were made for late-80s radio and MTV, all strong hooks, striking atmosphere, and immediate choruses. Fleetwood Mac were a 1970s giant, but *Tango in the Night* let them move through the MTV age without looking stranded in another decade.

That is also why the album sits in an interesting middle ground. It is plainly mainstream pop, but its chilly textures, sharp editing, and stylised surfaces borrow from the cooler end of new wave. It feels expensive and accessible, yet slightly uncanny too.

### Hit record, long afterlife 🌌
Commercially, *Tango in the Night* was enormous, the band’s biggest seller since *Rumours*. In the UK it had an especially long chart life, and in the US it spun off a run of major singles. Critics responded well to its craft and sound, even if the tension behind the scenes was hard to ignore.

Its legacy has only grown. This is one of the clearest examples of a classic rock band remaking itself for the 1980s without losing its emotional core. Beneath the lacquered production, you still hear the old Fleetwood Mac themes, longing, bruised romance, and beautiful unease. That mix is why the album still feels so alive.

## Singles (The U.A. Years+) by Dr. Feelgood 🎸

### ⚙️ A compilation built from fast, hard-working singles

Released in 1989 on Liberty/EMI, *Singles (The U.A. Years+)* looks back to Dr. Feelgood’s key run of 1974 to 1979 singles for United Artists. It is a compilation rather than a new studio album, but it works like a sharp history of the band’s first great phase, from the Wilko Johnson years into the early post-Wilko line-up. The “+” matters too, because it stretches past the earliest classics and shows how the band kept their bite after a major personnel change.

What gives these recordings their punch is how close they were to the stage act. Dr. Feelgood were a pub rock band in the truest sense, tight from constant gigging, economical in the studio, and not interested in dressing the songs up. “She Does It Right” has one of the best origin stories in their catalogue, Wilko Johnson wrote it the night before a BBC Radio 1 *Top Gear* session. That sort of speed tells you everything about their method.

### 🔥 The sound, all snap, drive and zero waste

Dr. Feelgood stripped blues rock down to its bones. Where plenty of 70s rock was getting bigger, smoother and more album-centred, they went the other way. These songs are short, tough and wired straight into rhythm.

Wilko Johnson’s guitar is the signature. He played jagged, chopping figures with a clipped, percussive attack, often avoiding long solos altogether. That left room for the engine underneath him, John B. Sparks on bass and The Big Figure on drums, who keep everything moving with a hard, dry pulse. Lee Brilleaux then comes crashing in with that rasping voice, half R&B shouter, half street-corner bruiser.

Tracks like “Roxette”, “Back in the Night” and “Going Back Home” sound as if they are pushing the tape machine along with them.

### 📻 Against album rock, and right on punk’s doorstep

This music landed in a decade dominated by album-oriented rock, when many major acts were treating the LP as the big artistic statement and filling records with polish, layers and extended playing time. Dr. Feelgood kept faith with the single, the riff and the backbeat.

That made them a natural fit for the mood that punk would soon turn into a full-scale revolt. They were not a punk band, but punk listeners heard something familiar in the attack, the lack of frills and the sense that any note not pulling its weight had to go. Their records helped clear space for that shift.

The production helped. Mid-70s studio technology could have made them glossy, but their records stay dry and immediate, with tape compression, tight mic sound and very little softness around the edges.

### 🏁 Reception, key songs and why it still matters

The compilation itself arrived during the CD-era rush to re-present important catalogues, and Dr. Feelgood were a perfect case. By then, their influence was easier to spot. Bands and songwriters from punk, mod revival and straight-ahead British rock had picked up ideas from them, especially that mix of blues roots and machine-like force.

Commercially, Dr. Feelgood were never about bloat or superstardom, but songs such as “Milk and Alcohol” gave them real reach, and this set gathers the run that built their reputation. As a snapshot of 70s British rock at its leanest, nastiest and most alive, *Singles (The U.A. Years+)* is a belting listen.

### Recording the ache: churches, Fort Apache and a bigger sound 🛠️

Released in March 1992, *Let Me Come Over* caught Buffalo Tom at exactly the right moment. The Boston trio had already built a name in indie and college-rock circles, but this was the record where their writing and sound locked into place. They made it with Sean Slade and Paul Q. Kolderie, the Fort Apache team whose fingerprints are all over early 1990s American alternative rock.

Part of the album was tracked at Dreamland in Hurley, New York, a converted church in the woods near Woodstock. Bill Janovitz later recalled how striking that space felt, and you can hear why it mattered. There is room in these songs. Even when the guitars hit hard, the record breathes. After that first burst of tracking, the band returned to Fort Apache in Cambridge for overdubs, shaping a sound that leaned more on acoustic texture than many listeners might expect from a grunge-era favourite.

That choice matters. Buffalo Tom asked for the acoustic guitars to sit prominently in the mix, which gave the album warmth without sanding off its rough edges. It still sounds like a power trio, but one with a bruised heart.

### Between grunge and college rock 🎶

Buffalo Tom were never a Seattle copy. *Let Me Come Over* sits in the same early 1990s wave as grunge, but its character is different. The guitars have weight, the rhythms have force, yet the songs care just as much about melody as impact. You can hear power-pop instincts, indie looseness and a very American emotional plainness all at once.

“Taillights Fade” became the signature song for good reason. It has the sweep of an anthem, but it never turns bombastic. That balance runs through the whole album. Buffalo Tom could go loud, then turn inward, and neither mode felt false. In a decade full of extremes, they made a record that allowed tenderness and noise to share the same room.

### Reception, reissue and why it lasted 📀

At the time, the album helped push Buffalo Tom from admired underground band to one of alternative rock’s most reliable names. Over the years, it has often been treated as their defining release, the one that best captures what they do.

The 25th anniversary edition, released in 2017, gave the album another well-earned look. Alongside the original LP, it added a 1992 live set from ULU in London, the band’s first official live release. That was a smart choice, because Buffalo Tom always had a direct, unflashy live power that suited these songs.

### Indie spirit and a quieter kind of defiance 🔥

What still feels fresh is how *Let Me Come Over* refuses rock macho clichés. These are not songs hiding behind irony or swagger. Janovitz writes plainly about love, distance and uncertainty, and the band treats those feelings as worthy of real volume.

That is where its indie spirit lives. Buffalo Tom used the tools of loud rock, but they did not chase empty aggression. In the middle of the alternative explosion, they made something human-sized, melodic and emotionally open. That is why the album still lands.

## *Mad Dogs & Englishmen* by Joe Cocker 🐶🎤

### 🎙️ A chaotic tour became a classic live album

Released in August 1970, *Mad Dogs & Englishmen* came out of a mad rush. Joe Cocker had headed to Los Angeles in March that year to put together a new touring group, and Leon Russell helped assemble a huge cast of players and singers in a matter of days. By the time the tour rolled out, the travelling party had swollen to more than 40 people, part rock band, part soul revue, part gospel caravan.

Although the album is usually linked to the Fillmore East shows on 27 and 28 March 1970, it was not simply lifted from one set of tapes. Parts also came from rehearsals at the A&M Soundstage in Los Angeles and from a later performance at Santa Monica Civic Auditorium. Glyn Johns later remixed the material, and some choir parts were repaired in the studio. That matters, because the record sounds wild and spontaneous, yet it was carefully shaped into that form. It is a live album with theatre behind it.

### 🎹 Soul, gospel and rock in a giant communal rush

What makes *Mad Dogs & Englishmen* so distinctive is its scale and feel. This is not tidy British blues-rock. It is a sprawling American-style revue, thick with gospel voices, horns, piano, organ and a rhythm section that pushes everything forward with a rough, human swing. Cocker sings as if every line is being dragged out of him, and the band answers him like a congregation.

Leon Russell’s presence is all over the sound. His piano and arranging bring a rolling, churchy energy, while the backing singers turn songs into call-and-response dramas. “The Letter” is the obvious example, punched up into something far bigger than the original. “Space Captain” captures the group’s loose grandeur just as well.

The guitar work does not chase flashy virtuosity. Instead, it helps build the mass of the sound, locking in with the bass and drums to keep the songs moving while keyboards, horns and choir add heat around the edges. That balance gives the album its force.

### 📈 A hit record in the age of album rock

The album was a major success, reaching No. 2 in the US and No. 16 in the UK. “The Letter” became a hit single and helped push the record past the million-sales mark. At a time when album-oriented rock was growing, *Mad Dogs & Englishmen* fit perfectly. Big live statements mattered in 1970, and a double LP like this felt like an event rather than a stopgap release.

### 🕰️ Its place in the 70s, and what came after

The album caught a moment when rock was opening out, borrowing freely from soul, R&B and gospel. It belongs to the same early-70s world that loved communal live performance and expansive arrangements. Later in the decade, punk would sneer at this sort of excess, the giant band, the big tour, the polished live myth-making. Still, punk’s taste for raw energy was not so far from Cocker’s full-throated abandon.

That is part of the album’s afterlife. It is both a document of live-rock bigness and a reminder that emotion can cut through any amount of production.

## Greatest Hits by The Cure 🎧

    layout={{
        imgs: [

### A farewell to Fiction, done Robert Smith’s way 🖤
Released in November 2001, *Greatest Hits* arrived as The Cure’s final commitment to Fiction Records, but it was not tossed out as a routine label clean-up. Robert Smith agreed on one condition: he would choose the songs himself. That matters, because the album feels less like a corporate summary and more like the band writing its own history.

The set pulls together singles from the late 1970s through to 2001, tracing the journey from the wiry ache of “Boys Don’t Cry” and “A Forest” to the bright rush of “Just Like Heaven”, “Lullaby”, “Lovesong” and “Friday I’m in Love”. Two new songs were added, “Cut Here” and “Just Say Yes”, giving the collection a present-tense pulse instead of treating it as a museum piece. The tracks were remastered by Tim Young at Metropolis, and early editions came with a bonus *Acoustic Hits* disc, with the same songs newly re-recorded in stripped-back form. Former drummer Boris Williams even returned for those sessions, a lovely bit of Cure lore.

### Why the music still feels so singular 🌙
What makes *Greatest Hits* distinctive is how clearly it maps The Cure’s unusual balance of opposites. They could sound gloomy and catchy at once, intimate and huge, fragile and pop-savvy. Few bands moved this easily between post-punk tension, new wave shimmer, guitar pop sweetness and slow-burning melancholy without losing their identity.

The acoustic companion disc makes that point even more strongly. Remove the studio gloss, the effects and the famous production touches, and the songs still hold together beautifully. That says a lot about Smith’s writing. These are not songs that depend on atmosphere alone. The melodies are strong, the emotional tone is unmistakable, and the romantic sadness never tips into self-parody.

### A millennial compilation for the CD and DVD age 💿
This was also a very 2001 release. *Greatest Hits* came out in an era when labels were trying to keep physical formats attractive as digital listening began to change buying habits. So The Cure did more than issue a basic one-disc anthology. There was the standard CD, the bonus acoustic disc, and a companion *Greatest Hits* DVD and VHS with videos.

That format strategy helped the album work for several audiences at once: older fans, casual buyers, and younger listeners just finding the band. Released only weeks after 9/11, it also landed in a moment when nostalgia, emotional directness and reflective music had a particular weight. The Cure’s blend of longing, romance and unease fit that mood quietly well.

### Reception, legacy and a different idea of rock history ✨
Commercially, the album did very well, reaching No. 19 in the UK and later earning 4× Platinum there. It also sold strongly in Australia and across Europe. Critics generally treated it as a smartly curated overview rather than a cynical cash-in, largely because Smith’s hand is so visible in the choices.

Its longer legacy lies in how it reframed the “greatest hits” album. This is not just a pile of familiar singles. It is a compilation, a remastering project, an acoustic reinterpretation, and a statement about what rock can contain. The Cure made vulnerability, oddness and melancholy central to pop success, and *Greatest Hits* makes that case in one sweep.

### Creation in tiny rooms, with a bigger plan 🛠️

First, a quick correction that matters here: **WHO ASKED? is not DOMi & JD Beck’s debut**. Their debut was **NOT TiGHT** in 2022. This album is the follow-up, released in **2026** on **Blue Note**.

That said, it still feels like a major arrival. After the guest-heavy sprawl of *NOT TiGHT*, DOMi and JD Beck made this one with a much tighter grip on the whole process. They have said they worked on it for more than two years, largely by themselves, in home setups in **Dallas and Los Angeles**. Much of it was recorded in **small rooms**, with demos built in **Logic** and tracking done in **Ableton**. They even described writing the record **orchestrally first**, which helps explain why it feels more shaped and intentional than their first album.

One lovely detail, if you are a gear and process nerd, is that they only really stepped into a conventional studio to record **Herbie Hancock’s piano**. Their own parts mostly came together in compact, DIY spaces. That is very 2020s, and very post-lockdown in spirit, even if this was not framed as a direct “pandemic album”.

### Jazz with sugar rush energy ⚡

Musically, *WHO ASKED?* is still jazz, but jazz in the way younger listeners often hear it now: open to **funk, hip-hop, electronic production, fusion, cartoon humour and absurd left turns**. DOMi’s keys and JD Beck’s drumming remain the centre of gravity, but this time the album leans more on **arrangement, vocals and sound design**.

What makes them distinctive is that the virtuosity never turns po-faced. JD Beck’s drumming can dart from hyperactive fills to deep-pocket groove in a blink, while DOMi’s playing flips between fusion flash, synth oddness and warm harmonic colour. The pair draw from jazz tradition, but not in a museum-piece way. You can hear the spirit of **fusion, rhythmic displacement, tight duo improvisation and quick conversational phrasing**, filtered through beat culture and internet-age speed.

### Reception, reputation and where it fits now 📀

Early reviews treated *WHO ASKED?* as a step forward in authorship. Writers picked up on the shift from cameo-driven excitement to something more self-defined. Commercially, firm chart data is thin, but the album arrived with real attention because the duo already had momentum from **Grammy-nominated** debut-era buzz.

Culturally, DOMi & JD Beck fit neatly into one of the most interesting things about contemporary music: **genre borders mean less than they used to**. This album is part of that story. It treats jazz as a live language that can share space with meme energy, DAW-based production and pop-adjacent presentation.

### Legacy in progress 🌍

Because the album is so recent, its long-term influence is still taking shape. Still, *WHO ASKED?* already says a lot about where modern jazz can go. It can be highly skilled without being stiff. It can be funny without becoming a joke. It can come from home-studio routines and still sound expansive.

That might be the real pull of DOMi & JD Beck. They make jazz feel less like a guarded club, and more like a fast-moving conversation anyone curious can join.

### A 40-year self-portrait 📚

Released in October 2023, *The Roaring Forty | 1983-2023* is less a standard greatest-hits set than a hand-built map of Billy Bragg’s career. Bragg began the story in 1983 with *Life’s a Riot with Spy vs Spy*, that lean, sharp debut where a Telecaster and a blunt point of view felt like enough to take on the world. This compilation marks four decades since then, and the scale is telling. There’s a concise 13-track version, a 40-track deluxe edition, and a huge 14-CD box with more than 300 recordings, from studio cuts to B-sides, sessions, live tracks, collaborations and previously unreleased material.

What gives the project its personality is that Bragg curated it himself. That matters. Instead of a label picking the obvious singles and calling it a day, he shaped it as a memoir in song. The box also includes a 64-page book built around 40 objects from his life and work, which turns the release into something close to an archive of a working musician’s conscience.

### Folk, punk and plain speaking 🪕  

Bragg has always lived in the overlap between folk, punk and rock, and this collection makes that plain. The acoustic tradition is there in the storytelling, the direct address and the belief that songs should carry news from ordinary life. But he never treats folk as a museum piece. His music has the attack of punk, even when the arrangement is spare. A solo voice and guitar can hit with the force of a band when the phrasing is this urgent.

That mix still feels fresh in a period when genre lines are loose. Bragg moved between protest song, indie rock, alternative folk and band-driven material long before “genre fluidity” became a common phrase. *The Roaring Forty* reminds you that he has been doing that for years without fuss, simply by following the song.

### Politics, pandemic echoes and present-day relevance ✊

This is a retrospective, not a pandemic-made studio album, so there is no big lockdown recording saga at its centre. Still, it arrives after the Covid years, when questions about solidarity, work, isolation and public life returned with force. Those themes have long been Bragg territory. Across these songs, working-class identity, labour politics, love, anti-racism and social justice keep reappearing.

That is why the set feels current rather than nostalgic. Bragg’s writing has always linked the personal and the political. He can write about romance, then turn a line so you notice the social pressure around it. He can sing about a picket line and make it feel human, not slogan-heavy.

### Reception and the long afterlife 🌍

Critics greeted the collection warmly, with praise for treating Bragg’s catalogue as a living body of work rather than a heritage product. Commercially, a UK Top 30 placing was a solid result for such an expansive archival release.

Its real value, though, is in showing Bragg’s influence with unusual clarity. You can hear how he helped make political songwriting feel immediate, how he bridged punk attitude with folk method, and how he kept storytelling rooted in everyday speech. *The Roaring Forty* is a reminder that Billy Bragg’s great subject has always been ordinary people trying to live decently in hard times, and that subject never goes out of date.

## *Duke* by Genesis 🎹

<LightGallery
    layout={{
        imgs: [
            { src: "/assets/2026-08-10-listened-to-this-week/albums/Duke.jpg", alt: "Duke by Genesis" },

### Recording the turning point 🛠️
Released in March 1980, *Duke* arrived at a moment when Genesis had fully settled into the trio of Phil Collins, Tony Banks and Mike Rutherford. They recorded it at Polar Studios in Stockholm in late 1979, then mixed it back in London at Maison Rouge, with David Hentschel co-producing. You can hear a band tightening its method. The long, wandering sprawl of 1970s Genesis had not vanished, but it had been edited, sharpened and pushed into stronger shapes.

One of the best little details from these sessions is the use of the Roland CR-78 drum machine on “Duchess”, the first time Genesis used one on a track. That matters because *Duke* catches the band at the exact point where old prog habits met new technology. The album still has suite-like thinking behind it, but the surfaces are cleaner, brighter and more modern.

### Prog brains, pop instincts 🎼
What makes *Duke* so distinctive is the way it balances direct songs with progressive design. “Misunderstanding” and “Turn It On Again” are compact, hooky and radio-ready, yet the record also includes “Duke’s Travels” and “Duke’s End”, which bring back the sweeping, dramatic side of the band. Even “Turn It On Again” has that odd Genesis touch, built around a tricky time feel that gives a hit single a faintly off-kilter pulse.

There is also a loose conceptual thread running through the album. It is not a strict story album in the old-school prog sense, but several songs connect in mood and sequence, creating a psychological arc of ambition, isolation and emotional confusion. That half-concept, half-song collection approach is part of its charm. It thinks like a prog record while moving like a pop record.

### Hits, critics and the road to the 1980s 📈
Commercially, *Duke* was a big leap forward. It became Genesis’s first UK number one album and reached number 11 in the US. “Turn It On Again” was a major UK hit, while “Misunderstanding” gave them one of their strongest US singles to that point. Critics have often treated it as the album where Genesis translated their earlier complexity into a broader rock and pop language without losing their personality.

That shift also helped prepare them for the MTV years, even though *Duke* arrived before MTV itself took over. The shorter songs, stronger hooks and polished production made Genesis much easier to carry into the visual pop era that would soon dominate the decade.

### Why *Duke* still matters ✨
Its legacy is easy to hear. *Duke* showed that a progressive band could survive the 1980s without pretending the previous decade had never happened. Synthesizers, drum machines and tighter arrangements did not flatten Genesis here, they refocused them. For many listeners, this is the album where the bridge between cult prog favourites and huge pop-rock stars became fully visible.
